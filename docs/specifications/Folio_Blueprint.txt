PART ONE
Product, Features & Design
Folio is a platform for readers. Not a book database, not a social network about 
books — a platform built around the full experience of being someone who 
reads: finding books, acquiring them, reading them, recording them, discussing 
them, and living a reading life. Goodreads treats books as inventory. Folio treats 
reading as an identity.
The core proposition: your library is the centre of your reading life, the AI is 
your guide, and community is what makes it worth turning up for.
1. Positioning & Brand
The difference: Goodreads is about books. Folio is about being a reader. That 
distinction gives the product permission to include things that would seem out 
of place on any existing platform: the tea you drink while reading, the bookshop 
you want to visit, the chair you read in, the translation you preferred, the 
afternoon you planned around finishing a novel. All of it coheres because it is all 
part of one person's relationship with reading.
The tone throughout — in the AI, in the interface copy, in community features — 
should feel warm, unhurried, and literate. Not a productivity app. Not a fitness 
tracker for books. Closer to a very good reading companion who happens to 
know everything about books and quite a lot about you.
Name: Folio. Clean, literary, connotes a page and a collection. Alternative 
candidates if taken: Stacks, Commonplace, Verso, The Shelf.
2. Navigation & Layout
Four primary navigation areas, accessible from a persistent bottom bar (mobile) 
or left sidebar (desktop):
● Chat — the AI reading companion; the entry point for almost everything
● Library — personal collection, all shelves, reading diary
● Browse — discovery, community content, reading paths, bookshops
● Community — forums, book clubs, creator profiles, Reading Life 
marketplace
The home screen on first open should be the Chat interface with a subtle 
summary card beneath it: what you are currently reading, your reading streak 
this week, and one gentle prompt. On mobile, the Library tab is the second 
most-visited surface and should load instantly.
Public homepage (logged-out): a curated feed of community content — reviews, 
reading diary entries, library builder essays, BookTok-style video. This 
communicates what the platform is before someone commits to signing up. 
Books and reading as a living culture, not a database.

3. The Library
The personal library is the heart of the product. Every book a user adds exists in 
a structured record with a rich set of possible states and metadata.
3.1 Shelves
Default shelves (always present, cannot be deleted):
● Reading Now — with progress tracking, start date, session log
● Read — with finish date, rating (optional), reread count
● Want to Read (TBR) — priority-ranked, with notes on who recommended 
it and why
● Owned But Unread — the guilt pile, separate from TBR
● Wishlist — want to buy, do not yet own
● Not For Me — decided against, with optional reason
● DNF (Did Not Finish) — abandoned, with note on page abandoned and 
why
● Interested / Watching — on the radar, not committed
● Lent Out — who has the copy and when it was lent
● Reference — books used non-linearly
Custom shelves: unlimited, user-defined. Examples include Favourites, Beach 
Reads, Book Club, Research, Gifts Received, Dad's Recommendations, Reread 
Candidates. Custom shelves can be private, friends-only, or public.
3.2 Gift Lists
Gift lists are a distinct shelf type tied to a named contact rather than the user 
themselves. Each contact in the user's reading circle has a profile: name, taste 
notes, genres they enjoy, authors they have mentioned, and — critically — 
books they already own, so the AI never recommends duplicates.
Gift list features:
● AI expansion: describe the person in natural language and the AI builds a 
ranked recommendation list, explains why each fits, and flags which are 
available in special or nice editions
● Priority tiers: perfect fit / might like / wildcard
● Occasion tagging: birthday, Christmas, graduation, new baby, etc.
● Claimed markers: shared lists show who has committed to buying which 
book, preventing duplicates across family members
● Shareable: users can share their own wishlist with a link so others can 
buy for them
● Budget-aware: AI filters and sorts by price, flags sales, finds secondhand 
copies
● Nice editions: separate mode for finding Folio Society, Penguin 
Clothbound, Everyman's Library, signed firsts, or illustrated editions of a 
specific title

3.3 Birthday & Occasion Reminders
Each contact in the reading circle can have a birthday or recurring occasion 
date. The platform:
● Sends a configurable reminder (2 to 8 weeks before, user-set)
● Opens a pre-populated suggestion from the contact's gift list filtered by 
current prices and stock
● Links directly to purchase, or flags for local collection
● In November and December: Christmas mode consolidates all upcoming 
gift lists into a single gift-planning view with total budget tracking and 
claimed/unclaimed status across all contacts
3.4 Book Records
Each book in the library carries a rich record:
● Edition and format owned (hardback, paperback, ebook, audiobook, large 
print)
● Where purchased or acquired
● Condition (for physical copies)
● Price paid
● Cover image (auto-fetched, user-replaceable)
● Tags, moods, themes (AI-suggested, user-editable)
● Private notes
● Public review (linked to profile)
● Reading journal entries (private, per session)
● Linked books (same series, same author, same theme)
● Translation and edition metadata (see section 3.5)
3.5 Editions, Translations & Versions
Translations and editions are first-class objects in the data model, not footnotes. 
The hierarchy is:
Work  →  Translation  →  Edition  →  Physical Copy
A user's relationship can attach at any level: loved the work, disliked the Fagles 
translation, want to try the Wilson, own the Lattimore, want a Folio Society 
edition. The AI understands these distinctions and uses them in 
recommendations.
Translator preferences are stored and used as recommendation inputs: "same 
translator I read for Anna Karenina" is a valid query. Edition contributors are 
tracked beyond translator: illustrator, editor, introduction writer, annotator, 
audiobook narrator.
Translation-aware queries the AI can handle:
● Which translation of Crime and Punishment would suit me?
● I found this translation too archaic — find me something more 
contemporary
● Which translation preserves the humour best?

4. Reading Diary & Session Tracking
Reading progress is tracked at the session level, not just the page level. Each 
reading session is a logged record: book, start page, end page, date, and 
optionally time. The AI can log sessions from natural language: "Read another 
42 pages before bed" — because it knows what the user is currently reading, no 
title is needed.
4.1 Automatically calculated analytics
● Pages read today / this week / this month / this year
● Average pages per reading day
● Average session size
● Days spent on each book
● Books finished per month and per year
● Current reading streak
● Pages remaining and estimated completion date
● Fastest and slowest reads
● Average time to finish a novel
● Abandoned-book statistics
4.2 Tone
The diary interface should feel like a beautiful record, not a fitness app. No 
aggressive streak notifications. The system might say "You've read 420 pages 
this week, mostly in three long weekend sessions." It does not say "YOU BROKE 
YOUR 137-DAY STREAK." Nudges are gentle and framed as observations, not 
performance metrics.
4.3 Time-aware recommendations
Once pace is established per genre, the AI can answer: "I've got about 45 
minutes — what could I read?" It knows this user reads literary fiction at 
roughly 28 pages per hour and can recommend something achievable, or 
suggest a good stopping point in their current read.
5. The Chat Interface
The AI reading companion is built on Claude and has full access to the user's 
library, reading diary, private context profile, local bookshops, and external 
data sources. It is the primary interface for most interactions — not a feature 
within the app, but the entry point.
5.1 Core capabilities
● Recommend — personalised, aware of what has already been read, never 
recommends duplicates

● Find — locates books at local libraries, nearby bookshops, online

● Compare — translations, editions, an author's earlier vs later work

6. Private Context Profile
The private context profile is information a user shares with the AI that shapes 
its behaviour silently and indefinitely. It is entirely separate from the public 
profile. No other user, no advertiser, and no platform analytics system can 
access it. The user has complete visibility and control over every item in it.
The design principle: the AI uses this information without referencing it unless 
the user brings it up first. If someone has recorded a bereavement, the AI 
simply does not recommend books featuring that kind of loss. It does not say "I 
know you've been through a difficult time." It just knows.

--- PAGE 7 ---
6.1 Life context
● Bereavement — who, and recency (a loss six weeks ago is different to six 
months ago)
● Illness — their own or someone close
● Pregnancy, miscarriage, or fertility difficulties
● Relationship breakdown
● Major stress (job loss, financial difficulty, caring responsibilities)
● Recovery (addiction, mental health, trauma)
6.2 Life structure
● Daily schedule and when they typically read
● Realistic uninterrupted reading time
● Whether they have children and roughly what age
● Work pattern (shift worker, 9-to-5, freelance, irregular)
● Commute type and whether they read on it
6.3 Taste and personality
● Whether they use reading to escape or to engage
● Whether they want to be challenged or comforted right now (can be 
updated)
● Whether they read for emotion, ideas, story, or language
● Their relationship with rereading
● How they feel about difficult or dark content — not a blanket filter, a 
calibration
6.4 Biography
● Where they grew up and have lived (affects how they read books set in 
those places)
● Languages they speak (relevant to translation vs original)
● Educational and professional background (affects what needs explaining)
● Family — children's ages for co-reading suggestions, partner's reading 
habits if shared
6.5 The Diary Layer
An open-ended private diary the user can write in freely. The AI reads entries as 
context, not instructions. Someone might write: "My dad died last Tuesday. I 
don't know what I want to read." The AI does not respond to this as a query. It 
simply knows, and the next recommendation is shaped accordingly — shorter, 
gentler, less demanding — without explanation.
Time-awareness: a bereavement flag does not mean permanent exclusion of 
grief from recommendations. The AI softens its avoidance gradually. If the user 
writes that they feel ready to engage with harder material, the AI responds to 
that.
6.6 Privacy architecture
● Total user control — every item visible, editable, deletable

● Not used for advertising, analytics, or any commercial purpose
● Local-first option: sensitive context stored on-device, sent to AI at session 
time only
● Diary entries encrypted at rest; not readable by the platform
● Completely separate from the public-facing social profile
7. Bookshops & Places
Bookshops are a third major entity in the platform, alongside books and people. 
The Places layer connects the digital reading life to physical locations.
7.1 Bookshop discovery
● Location-aware search: find independent bookshops near me, secondhand 
within 20 miles, antiquarian dealers, charity shops with book 
departments, specialist subject dealers
● Type classification: independent general, chain, secondhand, 
antiquarian/rare, specialist, academic, children's, comic/graphic novel, 
charity, market stall, online-only, publisher bookshop
● Bookshop profile pages: shops can claim their own listing, add stock 
information, post events, feature recommendations from their staff
● Saved shops: users save their home bookshops and favourite shops to 
visit
7.2 TBR-to-location connection
When a user is near a saved or relevant bookshop, the AI can flag books from 
their TBR that are likely stocked there. Advanced: shops that integrate stock 
data can enable exact inventory matching.
7.3 Reading places
Beyond bookshops: users can save cafés, libraries, parks, and other reading 
spots. The planning AI uses these: "You're in Oxford tomorrow — here are three 
bookshops worth visiting and a café nearby that works well for reading."
7.4 The GET THIS BOOK panel
On every book page, a unified acquisition panel:
● Borrow: local library availability, request from network
● Buy locally: nearby independent bookshops, chains
● Buy new online: Bookshop.org, publisher direct, Amazon
● Buy used: AbeBooks, Biblio, World of Books, local sellers
● Digital: ebook retailers, Internet Archive, Project Gutenberg
● Listen: Libro.fm (with local bookshop revenue share), Audible, LibriVox

10. The Reading Life Marketplace
Not merchandise. A marketplace for the objects, spaces, and rituals that make

what you already have. The second option prevents the marketplace from 
becoming lifestyle consumerism.

● Books & collecting: books, beautiful editions, secondhand books,

● Reading objects: bookmarks, book stands, reading lights, notebooks,

● Atmosphere: candles, tea, coffee, hot chocolate, mugs, blankets, cushions

Engineering Architecture
This section covers the technical systems required to deliver the product 
described in Part One. It is organised by domain: data models, AI systems,

12. Technology Stack
12.1 Frontend
● Framework: Next.js (React) with TypeScript — SSR for SEO on book and 
profile pages, CSR for interactive app surfaces
● Styling: Tailwind CSS with a custom design system; typography-led, 
warm palette
● Mobile: React Native (shared logic layer) for iOS and Android apps; or 
PWA-first with native wrapper if budget-constrained
● Rich text: TipTap (blog editor, journal entries); Markdown support 
throughout
● Video: native HTML5 player; FFmpeg for server-side transcoding
12.2 Backend
● API: Next.js API routes or a dedicated Node.js / Express service for 
heavier processing
● Database: PostgreSQL (primary relational store) — managed via 
Supabase or DigitalOcean Managed Databases
● Search: Typesense (self-hosted, fast, typo-tolerant) or Algolia for book, 
author, and community search
● Cache: Redis for session data, rate limiting, and hot query caching
● Queue: BullMQ (Redis-backed) for background jobs: import processing, 
notification dispatch, AI enrichment, price alerts
● File storage: AWS S3 (eu-west-2) for cover images, user uploads, video
● CDN: Cloudflare — static assets, edge caching, DDoS protection
12.3 AI
● Primary model: Claude via Anthropic API (chat interface, 
recommendations, session logging, gift suggestions, reading path 
generation, kit building)
● Embeddings: text-embedding-3-small (OpenAI) or equivalent for semantic 
similarity in recommendations
● Vector store: pgvector (Postgres extension) for embedding storage and 
similarity search
● Prompt management: structured system prompts with user context 
injection at query time

14. AI Systems
14.1 Context injection architecture
Every AI query assembles a structured context object before calling the API. 
This is the core of what makes the system work — the AI is never queried cold.
AI context object (assembled per query):    user.library_summary     -- shelves, 
recent reads, current book + page     -- reading pace per genre, average session 
length     -- TBR list (top 20 by priority)     -- DNF patterns, abandoned books 
user.taste_profile     -- genre weights, theme preferences     -- translator 
preferences     -- mood preferences (current vs historical)     -- explicit 
dislikes and avoidances    user.private_context  [encrypted, injected at 
runtime]     -- life context flags     -- recent diary entries (last 5, 
summarised)     -- current reading mode (comfort|challenge|escape)    
user.location_context     -- saved bookshops and libraries     -- current 
location (if permitted)     -- upcoming travel (if entered)    
user.social_context     -- gift contacts and their profiles     -- active book 
clubs and current reads     -- Reading Together partners
14.2 Recommendation engine
Recommendations use a hybrid approach: semantic similarity via embeddings, 
collaborative filtering from anonymised community data, and constraint-based 
filtering from the user's context.
● Step 1: Candidate generation — retrieve semantically similar works via 
pgvector from books the user has rated highly
● Step 2: Collaborative filter — weight candidates that users with similar 
taste profiles also read and enjoyed
● Step 3: Constraint filtering — remove already-read, already-owned, on 
TBR, not-for-me, and context-excluded titles
● Step 4: Claude ranking — the filtered candidate list is passed to Claude 
with the full context object; Claude re-ranks and writes natural-language 
explanations
● Step 5: Diversity check — ensure the final set spans authors, 
nationalities, publication periods, and tones
14.3 Translation recommendation
A specialist sub-system for recommending translations of specific works:
● Retrieves all known translations with their translator's other work
● Pulls community sentiment by translation (translation-specific review 
threads)
● Applies user's stored translator preferences
● Considers user's stated reading goals (literary fidelity, readability, 
contemporary voice, humour)
● Can cross-reference what users who disliked a given translation read 
instead
14.4 Reading session logging (NLP)
Natural language session logging requires reliable entity extraction:
● Current book detection: because the system knows what the user is 
actively reading, no title is needed in most log queries

--- PAGE 17 ---
● Page extraction: handles "read to page 214", "stopped at 214", "another 
42 pages", "finished chapter 12"
● Time extraction: "before bed", "this morning", "21:10 to 22:05"
● Ambiguity resolution: if the user has two active books, the system asks 
which
● Confirmation: always confirms the logged session before committing
14.5 Planning and scheduling AI
The "reading afternoon" feature and related planning queries require 
synthesising multiple data sources:
● Reading pace database (per user, per genre)
● Pages remaining in current book
● Weather API (with permission)
● Saved places (bookshops, cafés, libraries)
● TBR list (what comes next, local availability)
● User schedule from private context profile
● Output: a natural language suggested plan, not a rigid itinerary
14.6 Gift recommendation AI
Gift queries inject the contact's profile rather than (or in addition to) the user's 
own taste:
● Contact taste notes, known owned books, occasion, budget
● Platform community data: what readers with similar profiles to the 
contact enjoyed
● Edition and price data: nice editions, current prices, secondhand 
availability
● Deduplication: cross-references all past gift lists for that contact
14.7 Sensory reading kit generation
Kit generation is a creative synthesis task:
● Book metadata: setting, period, mood, themes, nationality
● User inventory (from private context): what they already own that could 
be incorporated
● Marketplace inventory: available items that fit the theme and the user's 
budget
● Always generates a "use what you have" version before offering a 
purchase path
15. Privacy & Security Architecture
15.1 Data tiers
● Tier 1 — Public: shelves, reviews, reading stats, profile. Standard web 
security practices.

--- PAGE 18 ---
● Tier 2 — Private (server-side): library data, session logs, gift lists. User-
only access, server-side encryption at rest.
● Tier 3 — Sensitive (encrypted): private context profile, diary entries. 
Encrypted with user-derived key. Platform cannot read in plaintext.
15.2 Encryption for Tier 3
● User diary entries and context profile fields encrypted client-side before 
transmission using AES-256-GCM
● Encryption key derived from user password + server-side salt via 
PBKDF2 or Argon2; server never sees the key
● At AI inference time: client decrypts context, constructs the AI payload 
locally or via a sealed server function that cannot log the plaintext
● Local-first option: Tier 3 data stored only in device secure storage (iOS 
Keychain, Android Keystore); never sent to the server except as 
encrypted payload at inference time
15.3 Private context — what it is never used for
● Advertising targeting — hard prohibition, enforced architecturally (it is 
never accessible to the ad or analytics system)
● Platform analytics or aggregate reporting
● Visible to any other user
● Accessible by platform staff without explicit user consent and legal 
process
15.4 Authentication
● Auth.js (NextAuth) — email/password, OAuth (Google, Apple)
● Two-factor authentication: TOTP via authenticator app
● HTTP-only cookies, CSRF protection, secure flags
● SSO/SAML available for institutional accounts (libraries, publishers)
16. Integration Engineering
16.1 Book metadata pipeline
Book metadata is assembled from multiple sources and merged into the

Library > Google Books > Wikidata > publisher-submitted data.
● ISBN lookup: ISBNdb or Open Library API on book add; triggers full 
metadata fetch
● Barcode scanning: mobile camera → ISBN → metadata fetch (500ms 
target)
● Wikidata: translator data, edition relationships, award data via SPARQL 
queries
● Open Library: work/edition/translation hierarchy, cover images
● Google Books: supplementary metadata, preview links

● Enrichment queue: after initial add, background job enriches with full

16.2 Goodreads import
The most critical import path. Goodreads provides a CSV export. The importer:
● Parses the CSV, maps Goodreads shelves to Folio shelf structure
● Resolves each book to the canonical work via ISBN or title/author 
matching
● Preserves ratings, dates, review text, and custom shelf assignments
● Runs as a background job; user sees progress and can continue using the 
platform
● Handles duplicates and ambiguous matches with a review queue
● Target: full import of a 500-book library in under 2 minutes
